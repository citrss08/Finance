Sources of finance

Capital expenditure buys non-current assets (machines, buildings). Revenue expenditure covers day-to-day costs (wages, rent). Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. It pays the daily bills.

Evaluate tip: match the finance to the need and the time frame. Overdrafts suit short gaps. Loans and shares suit long-term assets. A firm already highly geared should not borrow more.

Costs, revenue and break-even

Total cost (TC) = FC + VC
Revenue (TR) = P × Q
Profit = TR − TC
Contribution per unit = P − VC
Break-even output = FC ÷ (P − VC)
Margin of safety = actual − break-even
Target output = (FC + target profit) ÷ (P − VC)

Fixed costs (FC) stay the same as output changes (rent, insurance). Variable costs (VC) rise with output (materials). Contribution is what each unit adds towards covering fixed costs. Once fixed costs are covered, each extra unit adds profit.

Worked example

P = $20, VC = $8, FC = $60,000.
Contribution = 20 − 8 = $12. Each unit sold gives $12 towards the fixed costs.
Break-even = 60,000 ÷ 12 = 5,000 units.
Selling 6,500 units: margin of safety = 6,500 − 5,000 = 1,500 units, so sales can fall by 1,500 units before a loss.
Profit at 6,500 = 1,500 × 12 = $18,000.
For $24,000 profit: (60,000 + 24,000) ÷ 12 = 7,000 units.

Advantages

  • Simple to draw and understand
  • Shows risk and the margin of safety
  • Useful for loan requests and pricing decisions

Limitations

  • Assumes straight lines: no discounts, no bulk-buy savings
  • Assumes everything produced is sold
  • Costs and prices are assumed to stay fixed
  • Hard to use for firms with many products

Key terms

  • Cost of goods sold (COGS): direct cost of the goods sold, such as ingredients.
  • Gross profit: revenue − COGS. It shows how well the product is priced and produced.
  • Operating profit: gross profit − expenses. It shows profit from normal trading.
  • Net profit: what is left after interest and tax. It can be paid out as dividends or retained.
  • Non-current assets: used for more than a year. Current assets: turned into cash within a year (inventory, receivables, cash).
  • Current liabilities: due within a year. Non-current liabilities: due after a year (long-term loans).
  • Equity: the owners' stake: share capital plus retained profit.

The income statement shows performance over time. The balance sheet shows position at one moment. Accounts are a snapshot and can hide things, such as window dressing.

ROCE in detail

Capital employed = equity 400 + non-current liabilities 200 = 600. ROCE shows how efficiently the firm uses all its long-term funds. Compare it with the interest rate on borrowing: if ROCE is 20% and loans cost 8%, borrowing to invest is worthwhile. If a bank deposit pays 3%, a 20% ROCE is strong.

ROCE advantages

  • Compares firms of different sizes
  • Compares with interest rates and past years
  • Shows if funds are used well

ROCE limitations

  • Depends on how assets are valued and depreciated
  • A snapshot: capital employed can be inflated or deflated
  • Ignores risk, non-financial factors and the industry

Improve profitability: raise prices, cut COGS (cheaper suppliers), cut expenses, increase sales volume. Improve liquidity: chase receivables, reduce inventory, delay payables, use finance with a longer term.

Liquidity guide: a current ratio of 1.5 to 2 and an acid test near 1 is often healthy. Too high means money is sitting idle. Too low risks failing to pay debts. It depends on the industry (a supermarket needs less than a manufacturer).

Limits of ratio analysis: uses past data, depends on accounting policies, ignores qualitative factors, and only helps when compared with past results, competitors or targets.

Gearing shows how much of long-term funding is borrowed. Over 50% is often called highly geared. Higher gearing means more risk (fixed interest, even when profit falls), but lower cost and no loss of control. Lower gearing is safer but may mean slower growth.

Efficiency: Sunrise holds stock for 68 days (long for a bakery, so waste is likely), collects from customers in 32 days and pays suppliers in 46 days. Customers pay faster than the bakery pays, which helps cash flow. Improve it: shorter inventory days (just-in-time), shorter debtor days (early-payment discounts), longer creditor days (negotiate terms, without harming supplier relationships).

Cash flow forecasting

Cash is money available now. Profit is revenue minus costs and can be unpaid. A profitable firm can fail when customers pay late and bills come first. This is a liquidity problem.

Net cash flow = inflows − outflows
Closing = opening + net cash flow

January: 40,000 − 45,000 = −5,000, so the closing balance is 10,000 − 5,000 = 5,000. February opens with that 5,000. The balance is positive, but falling to 2,000 in March means a risk.

Advantages

  • Shows shortfalls early so action can be taken
  • Helps with bank loans and investor pitches
  • Helps with planning purchases and payments

Limitations

  • Based on estimates, which can be wrong
  • Unexpected events (a supplier problem, a rival) break it
  • Needs updating regularly

Fixing a shortfall: overdraft or short-term loan, chase receivables, delay payables, sell assets, cut costs. Surplus: invest, pay down debt, pay dividends.

Investment appraisal

Project: invest $100,000. Returns: Year 1 $30,000, Year 2 $40,000, Year 3 $50,000, Year 4 $40,000.

Payback period

After year 2: 30 + 40 = 70,000 recovered. Still need 30,000, and year 3 brings 50,000. So 2 + 30 ÷ 50 = 2.6 years (2 years 7 months, since 0.6 × 12 = 7.2).

Average rate of return (ARR)

Total returns 160,000 − 100,000 = 60,000 profit. ÷ 4 years = 15,000 a year. 15,000 ÷ 100,000 × 100 = 15%.

Also consider qualitative factors: risk, ethics, the environment, effect on staff, and whether the forecasts are reliable.

Budgets and variance analysis (HL)

A budget is a financial plan for a future period. It sets targets, controls spending, and lets managers delegate.

Variance = actual − budget

Favourable (F): better for profit (higher revenue or lower costs). Adverse (A): worse for profit (lower revenue or higher costs).

BudgetActualVariance
Revenue100,00092,0008,000 A
Costs70,00075,0005,000 A
Profit30,00017,00013,000 A

Revenue was 8,000 lower, which is bad. Costs were 5,000 higher, which is also bad. Total: 8,000 + 5,000 = 13,000 adverse. Managers should investigate why.

Advantages

  • Gives clear targets and motivation
  • Helps control costs and spot problems
  • Helps allocate resources

Disadvantages

  • Forecasts can be wrong, making budgets rigid
  • Can cause conflict between departments
  • Takes time, and managers may inflate costs

Extra finance concepts

Depreciation

Non-current assets lose value through wear and age. Depreciation spreads the cost over the useful life. Net book value = cost − accumulated depreciation.

Straight line: (cost − residual value) ÷ life. A $50,000 machine, residual $5,000, 5 years: (50,000 − 5,000) ÷ 5 = $9,000 a year.
Reducing balance (20%): year 1 = 20% × 50,000 = 10,000, so NBV = 40,000. Year 2 = 20% × 40,000 = 8,000. The charge is higher early on.

Interest and the time value of money

Simple interest: $1,000 at 5% for 3 years = 3 × 50 = $150. Compound interest: 1,000 × 1.05³ = $1,157.63, because interest is earned on interest. This is why NPV discounts future money: $1 today can be invested to grow.

Other terms

  • Intangible assets: no physical form (patents, brands). Goodwill is the value above the net assets when a firm is bought.
  • Window dressing: making accounts look better than they are (for example, delaying purchases before the year end). It is legal only if it stays within accounting rules, but it can be unethical.
  • Dividends: profit paid to shareholders. Retained profit: kept in the business.
  • Inflation reduces the real value of money, which affects budgets and investment returns.

Practice questions with step-by-step working

Try each one on paper first, then open the working. These are written in IB style for practice. They are not copies of real past papers.

Q1. Break-even [4 marks]

A firm has fixed costs of $90,000. It sells at $25 a unit and variable cost is $10 a unit. (a) Calculate the break-even output. (b) Calculate the profit if it sells 7,500 units.

Show working

(a) Contribution = 25 − 10 = $15. Break-even = 90,000 ÷ 15 = 6,000 units.
(b) Revenue = 7,500 × 25 = 187,500. Total cost = 90,000 + (7,500 × 10) = 165,000. Profit = 187,500 − 165,000 = $22,500.
Check: margin of safety = 1,500 units × $15 = $22,500 ✓.

Q2. Ratios [6 marks]

Revenue $400k, cost of goods sold $240k, expenses $100k. Current assets $90k (including inventory $30k), current liabilities $60k. (a) Calculate the gross profit margin. (b) Calculate the operating profit margin. (c) Calculate the current ratio and the acid test ratio. (d) Comment on its liquidity.

Show working

(a) Gross profit = 400 − 240 = 160. 160 ÷ 400 × 100 = 40%.
(b) Operating profit = 160 − 100 = 60. 60 ÷ 400 × 100 = 15%.
(c) Current ratio = 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5. Acid test = (90 − 30) ÷ 60 = 1.0.
(d) A current ratio of 1.5 and an acid test of 1.0 suggest the firm can just meet its short-term debts without selling inventory. It is acceptable, but with little room for error. It should be compared with competitors and past years.

Q3. Investment appraisal [6 marks]

A project costs $80,000. Returns: Year 1 $20,000, Year 2 $30,000, Year 3 $40,000, Year 4 $30,000. (a) Calculate the payback period. (b) Calculate the ARR. (c) Explain one limitation of using payback.

Show working

(a) After year 2: 20 + 30 = 50,000. Remaining = 30,000 out of year 3's 40,000. 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75. Payback = 2.75 years (2 years 9 months).
(b) Total returns = 120,000. Profit = 120,000 − 80,000 = 40,000. Average = 40,000 ÷ 4 = 10,000. ARR = 10,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 12.5%.
(c) Payback ignores cash flows after the payback point (here, Year 4's $30,000), so a project with large later returns could be wrongly rejected.
